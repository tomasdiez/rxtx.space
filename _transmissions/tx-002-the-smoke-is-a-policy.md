Most evenings in south Bali the air carries two smokes. The first is incense — the morning offerings, a smell the island has carried for centuries. The second is plastic. It rises from the burning piles at the edge of the rice fields and the corners of the banjar, it arrives around dusk, and everyone who lives here has learned to tell the difference with their eyes closed.

The second smoke is new, and it is not an accident. Since August 2025, when the Suwung landfill — Bali's biggest open dump, thirty-two hectares, four decades of the island's trash — stopped accepting organic waste, burning has become the system. The full closure has been postponed four times now. A national law required every open dump in Indonesia to close by 2013; enforcement arrived more than a decade late, and the infrastructure never arrived at all. More than a thousand tonnes of waste a day, and an instruction to "manage it at source" with no tools provided. At source turned out to mean: in a barrel, with a match.

This is what it looks like when pollution is produced by policy. Not emitted by a factory the state failed to regulate — produced by the state itself, in the gap between a decree and its budget. Economic forces do the rest: an island whose economy sells paradise cannot afford to admit the smoke, so the smoke goes unmeasured. What isn't measured isn't happening.

And the smoke does not check passports. It settles in the lungs of the Balinese grandmother sweeping her family compound, of the construction crew building the next villa, of the kid riding pillion to the international school down the road. PM2.5 is the most democratic substance on the island — it treats every resident exactly the same, whatever their nationality. Which is more than the policy does.

A government that cannot measure cannot be held accountable, and a government that will not measure has made its choice. The official map of Bali's air is close to blank. So the question becomes a civic one: who owns the instrument?

A decade ago in Barcelona we built Smart Citizen and ran Making Sense, an EU project on participatory environmental sensing, and I co-wrote the toolkit that came out of it. The lesson was simple: a sensor on a balcony is not a gadget — it is a citizen deciding that environmental data should not only flow from the top down. I did not expect to be re-reading that toolkit here, in Bali, as a field manual. But here we are.

So: Making Sense Bali. The principle is the cheapest honest node the Indonesian supply chain allows — a microcontroller and a climate sensor ordered on Tokopedia for the price of two lunches, fifteen to twenty-five dollars; a second tier adds a particulate sensor for around fifty. Smart Citizen Kits serve as the reference backbone, and every cheaper node is calibrated upward against them, honest about its fidelity. Two kits are already transmitting, the DIY builds are in workshops, and the hardware is being replicated in Bali and Tangerang. Residents' reports arrive over WhatsApp, because that is where the community already lives. More deployments land in the coming weeks.

Cheap is not a compromise here — cheap is the strategy. A single precise sensor gives you a measurement; a hundred rough ones give you a map: which street, which evening, which fire. Spatial density is what turns "south Bali had elevated PM" into "this banjar, on Wednesday evenings," and only the second sentence can start an argument that wins.

The goal is not to replace regulatory monitoring. It is to make the invisible debatable — to put evidence within reach of the people breathing the consequences. If the state can produce pollution by decree, residents have to produce evidence by hand. Not to shame anyone: to let the policy finally meet its own output, in numbers a banjar, a school, or the province itself can act on.

Status, honestly: early. Everything is open — hardware, firmware, data, the playbook — at [github.com/mdg-bali/makingsensebali](https://github.com/mdg-bali/makingsensebali). Fork it. Call it Making Sense your-city. The smoke is already everywhere; the instruments should be too.

TX-002, logged from the Bukit in Bali, where tonight the air carries both smokes.
